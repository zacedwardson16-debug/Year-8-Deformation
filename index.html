Forces can change the shape of an object by stretching, squashing (compressing), bending or twisting it. This is called deformation. To change an object’s shape you need two forces acting in opposite directions.

Why two forces?

If you pull one end of a spring and nothing holds the other end, the whole spring just moves towards you. To stretch it, something must also pull the other end the opposite way. When a spring hangs from a clamp, the weight pulls the bottom down and the clamp pulls the top up.

Hooke’s law

For a spring, the extension is directly proportional to the force: double the force and the extension doubles. The graph is a straight line through the origin. This is Hooke’s law. It only works up to the elastic limit. Past the elastic limit the line curves, and the spring will not go back to its original length.

A spring that obeys Hooke’s law can measure forces: this is how a newtonmeter works. Spring A stretches 2 cm for every 1 N. Spring B stretches 1 cm for every 1 N. Both are 5.0 cm long with nothing on them. Hang each object and work out its weight.

Stiff and stretchy springs · challenge

For the same force, Spring B stretches less: it is stiffer. The spring constant, k, measures stiffness: F = k × e, with F in newtons and e in metres. Spring A: k = 1 N ÷ 0.02 m = 50 N/m. Spring B: k = 1 N ÷ 0.01 m = 100 N/m. A bigger spring constant means a stiffer spring.
